Technical removals: no drop zone, no margin

Short answer

A removal is technical when the tree can't be felled and can't be dropped in pieces — so every piece is rigged and lowered instead. Height barely matters; what drives the price is whether there's a drop zone, whether the chipper can reach, and what's underneath. Before anyone starts, ask for a certificate of insurance sent from their carrier naming your address, and ask them to tell you specifically how the wood is leaving your property. Vague answers on that second question are where surprise invoices come from.

What makes a removal technical

A technical removal is one where the tree can't be felled and can't be dropped in pieces, so every piece has to be controlled on the way down. The usual reasons, in rough order of how often I see them: there's a structure or a pool underneath, the lot falls away and there's no flat ground to work from, the only access is a narrow drive or a gate, or the tree is compromised enough that a climber can't trust it and the work has to be done from outside it.

None of that is about size. A 25-foot hackberry hanging over a glass sunroom is a technical removal. A 70-foot pecan in an open pasture is an easy afternoon. Height is the number homeowners quote me and it's the least useful one.

The reason this page exists is that technical work is where the quotes get strange. You'll get three numbers that don't resemble each other, and the spread is almost always about whether the person quoting has thought through how the wood leaves the property.

Why it's priced differently

Felling a tree into a drop zone is minutes of cutting. Dismantling the same tree on rigging lines is hours, because each piece is a separate small operation: get tied in above it, set a line, take the cut, control the descent, get it clear on the ground, repeat. A limb that would take fifteen seconds to drop takes several minutes to lower.

Then there's what happens on the ground. If the chipper can't get within reach, everything is carried, and carrying brush uphill out of a Hill Country back yard is slower than cutting it. If the wood can't be dragged, it gets cut into pieces a person can lift, which is more cuts and more trips.

And there's the risk premium, which is honest rather than padding. On a job where a mistake lands on a roof instead of a lawn, everything slows down: smaller pieces, more rigging, more checking. Slower is the whole point.

The general version of how I price work is on the what it costs page. Technical removals sit at the top of that range for the reasons above, and I quote them per tree after seeing them, never over the phone.

The Hill Country lot problem

West Lake Hills, Rollingwood, Bee Cave, Lakeway, Dripping Springs, and the west edge of Austin all share a shape: the house sits on the flat part and the lot falls away behind it, often with a deck out over the fall. The tree is usually below the house, which means the crown is at eye level from the living room and the base is twenty feet down a slope with no vehicle access.

On lots like that the questions are where a rope can be anchored, whether wood can be moved uphill at all, and whether the drive will take a truck and a chipper without taking the retaining wall with it. Those answers are the estimate.

One local note worth having: West Lake Hills and Rollingwood are separately incorporated cities, not part of Austin, so Austin's tree ordinance is not the one that applies to you. Their city halls set their own rules and thresholds. Ask them directly before you plan a removal — I've verified Austin, Kyle, Buda and Dripping Springs on the permits page and I won't summarize an ordinance I haven't read.

What to ask anybody, including me

This is the work where the difference between companies matters most, and there are four questions that sort it out quickly.

Can I have a certificate of insurance sent from your carrier? Not shown on a phone, sent from the agent, with your address on it. General liability and workers' compensation. If a limb goes through your roof, that certificate is the entire difference between a repair and a lawsuit.

How is the wood coming out? A specific answer — rigged to this spot, carried out through that gate, chipper on the street — tells you they've walked the job. A vague answer means they haven't, and the price will change once they start.

Who is going up the tree, and what are they tied in with? You want to hear about a climbing system, a second point of attachment when cutting, and a helmet. Spikes on a tree you're keeping is a red flag — climbing spurs puncture the cambium and are for removals, not pruning.

What's the plan if it goes wrong? Anybody who has done this work for a while has an answer, because rigging fails sometimes and the plan is what keeps that boring.

Cranes, honestly

Some removals are better done with a crane than with a climber: a dead, hollow or storm-broken tree that nobody should be tied into, or a big stem over a roof where picking pieces off cleanly beats lowering them. It's genuinely the right call sometimes.

I'm not going to tell you on a web page what equipment is on site for your job, because that depends on the job. What I will do is look at it and tell you straight whether it's work I'd take, work that wants a crane, or work you should put in front of somebody else — and I'd rather say the third one than learn it halfway up.

That's not a hedge, it's the answer to the question you're actually asking, which is whether the person who shows up will be honest about the limits of what they should be doing over your house.

Questions I get asked

What is a technical tree removal?

A removal where the tree can't be felled or dropped in pieces, so each piece is rigged and lowered under control. Common causes are a structure, pool or driveway underneath, a steep lot with no working ground, access too narrow for equipment, or a tree too compromised for a climber to work inside safely.

Why is a removal over my house so much more expensive?

Because the work is hours of rigging rather than minutes of felling. Every limb is cut and lowered on a line as a separate operation, wood is often reduced to hand-carry size, and brush may have to be moved by hand to reach a chipper. The tree is the same tree; the day is several times longer.

What insurance should a tree company have?

General liability and workers' compensation, and you should ask for a certificate of insurance sent directly from their carrier or agent naming your property, not a photo of a card. On a removal over a structure this is the most important question you can ask.

Is it bad for a climber to use spikes on my tree?

On a tree you're keeping, yes. Climbing spurs puncture the bark and cambium at every step, leaving wounds up the trunk. They're standard and appropriate on a removal, where the tree is coming down anyway. Seeing them on a pruning job is a reason to ask why.

When does a tree need a crane instead of a climber?

Typically when nobody should be tied into it — a dead, hollow or storm-broken stem — or when picking large pieces off over a roof is safer than lowering them through a canopy. It's job-specific. Anybody looking at your tree should be willing to tell you if it's beyond what they should take on.
